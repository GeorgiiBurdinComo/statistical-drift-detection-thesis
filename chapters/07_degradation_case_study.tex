\chapter{Degradation Case Study}
\label{chap:degradation-case-study}

Over the monitoring period the alert rule fired once on evidence that survives scrutiny. On 22~March~2026 \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} lost 13 accuracy points on the fixed panel, and a further 15 the next day. It returned to its previous level within five days, without intervention.

This chapter documents that episode, establishes that it was specific to one model, and explains why it does not answer RQ5.

\section{The episode}
\label{sec:alerted-degradation-case}

Figure~\ref{fig:panel-trajectory} shows panel accuracy for every monitored model across the period, with \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} picked out.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \input{assets/drift/fig_panel_trajectory.tex}
    \caption{Accuracy on the frozen panel $\mathcal{D}_n$, 8~March to 27~July~2026, monitoring runs only. \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} in colour; the remaining monitored models in grey. The 22--23~March excursion is the only departure of this size anywhere in the period.}
    \label{fig:panel-trajectory}
\end{figure}

The trajectory is flat apart from two adjacent points. Panel accuracy runs at $0.797$ (8~March), $0.807$ (10~March) and $0.789$ (15~March), falls to $0.663$ on 22~March and $0.513$ on 23~March, and is back at $0.797$ on 28~March. It then stays between $0.787$ and $0.826$ for the remaining four months.

Against the 8~March baseline the two excursion days give $-13.1$pp and $-28.3$pp, both far beyond the corrected threshold $\alpha_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.0022$ of Section~\ref{sec:baseline-multiplicity}. Table~\ref{tab:contingency-trigger} gives the paired counts for the first of the two.

\input{assets/drift/contingency_trigger.tex}

Of the 297 items evaluated in both runs, 51 went from correct to wrong and 12 the other way, so $n_{\mathrm{dis}}=63$. The exact one-sided p-value is
\[
p_{\mathrm{exact}}=\sum_{k=51}^{63}\binom{63}{k}2^{-63}=3.73\times10^{-7},
\]
and the accuracy change is $(12-51)/297=-13.1$pp. The imbalance carries the evidence: a symmetric reshuffle of the same 63 items would leave accuracy unchanged and the test would not fire. On 23~March the counts are $b=91$, $c=6$ over $n=300$, giving $p_{\mathrm{exact}}=6.7\times10^{-21}$.

\section{The episode was specific to one model}
\label{sec:mcnemar-at-trigger}

All models are submitted in the same weekly job, against the same panel, with the same prompt template and the same parsing rule. A fault in any of those would move every model at once. Table~\ref{tab:control-same-week} applies the alert rule of Section~\ref{sec:baseline-multiplicity} to every monitored model over the same transition, each against its own 8~March baseline.

\input{assets/drift/control_same_week.tex}

Eleven of the twelve models move by between $-3.0$ and $+1.5$ percentage points; \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} moves by $-13.1$. The nearest other case is \texttt{gpt-5-mini} at $-3.0$pp with $p=0.047$, which falls below the nominal $\alpha$ but is two orders of magnitude short of the corrected threshold $\alpha_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.0023$; Section~\ref{sec:method-comparison} shows this model's negative offset to be an artefact of its baseline run.

Shared-cause explanations --- dataset corruption, a prompt or template change, a parser regression, a provider-wide incident --- all predict a common shift across the roster. None is consistent with one model moving by thirteen points while eleven others stay inside three. What remains is a change confined to one model endpoint, which is the case the monitoring design was built to catch.

This separation is only available because several models are evaluated in the same batch. A single-model monitor would have produced the same alert with no way to distinguish a provider-side change from a fault in the evaluation pipeline, and the correct operational response differs in the two cases.

\section{Transient versus sustained degradation}
\label{sec:transient-provider-degradation}

The episode recovered on its own. Accuracy was back to baseline on 28~March, five days after the first alert, and no corrective action was taken; the model was not the deployed one at the time. Nothing in the following four months repeats it.

This distinction matters operationally, because the two cases call for opposite responses. A transient dip that resolves within one evaluation cycle needs no action, and acting on it wastes an escalation and a switch. A sustained drop needs either a model switch or prompt repair. The monitoring rule as specified in Chapter~\ref{ch:drift} cannot tell them apart at the moment it fires: at 22~March, the transient and the sustained case look identical.

The practical consequence is that a single alert is not a sufficient trigger for action. It is a trigger for the escalation sequence of Section~\ref{sec:cost-aware-comparison}: re-evaluate on $\mathcal{D}_N$, prepare a fallback, and keep monitoring. Prompt optimisation is warranted only once the regression has been seen in more than one evaluation window. Requiring persistence also happens to suppress the \texttt{gpt-5-mini} false alert discussed in Section~\ref{sec:method-comparison}, which fired in a single window.

The re-evaluation step is not a formality. Had this episode persisted, the obvious fallback would have been \texttt{gpt-4.1-nano}, the neighbouring model from the same family, and the panel ranks \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} ten accuracy points above it. On the full benchmark the order is the other way round, $0.774$ against $0.753$ (Section~\ref{sec:expected-cost-snapshot}), while the expected-cost ranking at $R=5$ reverses again and favours \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini} on its much lower false-positive count. A fallback chosen from the panel alone would therefore have been chosen on a comparison that does not hold operationally, which is exactly why the escalation path leaves $\mathcal{D}_n$ before any switch is made.

\section{Prompt optimisation results}
\label{sec:prompt-optimisation-results}

Because the episode resolved before the escalation sequence completed, the prompt-repair branch of Figure~\ref{fig:run-decision-logic} was never entered on a live alert. GEPA was therefore run offline, to establish whether the mechanism produces usable gains on this task at all.

Table~\ref{tab:gepa-runs} reports the completed runs. Each splits the labelled data into disjoint train, validation and test parts, optimises against validation, and reports the selected candidate on held-out test. The second \texttt{gpt-4.1-nano} run is a warm start whose seed is the first run's selected prompt.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \footnotesize
    \caption{Completed GEPA runs. Validation drives candidate selection; the test column is held out. Splits are given as train/validation/test item counts.}
    \label{tab:gepa-runs}
    \begin{tabular}{llccccr}
    \toprule
    Model & Split & Seed val. & Best val. & Seed test & Best test & $\Delta$test \\
    \midrule
    \texttt{gpt-4.1-nano} & 719/239/241 & 0.766 & 0.795 & 0.784 & 0.768 & $-1.7$pp \\
    \texttt{gpt-4.1-nano} & 719/239/241 & 0.762 & 0.816 & 0.747 & 0.776 & $+2.9$pp \\
    \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} & 719/239/241 & 0.778 & 0.954 & 0.751 & 0.929 & $+17.8$pp \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Validation gains are positive in all three runs, between $2.9$ and $17.6$ points. Test gains are not: the first \texttt{gpt-4.1-nano} run gains $2.9$ points on validation and loses $1.7$ on test. Selecting on a 239-item validation split overfits that split, and the gain does not have to survive on held-out data.

A second effect limits how far these numbers can be pushed. The warm-start run re-evaluated the first run's selected prompt on the same validation split and scored it at $0.762$, whereas the first run had measured the identical prompt at $0.795$. That is a $3.3$-point spread on one prompt, one split, from inference nondeterminism alone --- the same order of magnitude as the validation gains being selected on. Candidate ranking on a split this size is therefore only partly a measurement of prompt quality.

Taken together: GEPA produced a large held-out gain on one model, a small one on another, and negative transfer on a third, with run-to-run noise comparable to the smaller effects. That is enough to show the mechanism can work and not enough to predict when it will.

Figure~\ref{fig:gepa-opt-curve} shows the search behaviour for the first run.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \input{assets/drift/fig_gepa_trajectory.tex}
    \caption{GEPA search on \texttt{gpt-4.1-nano} (run 20260713-184939Z). Validation score of each accepted candidate, with the running best. The seed scores $0.766$; candidate~2 reaches $0.795$ and is selected. Later accepted candidates fall below the incumbent.}
    \label{fig:gepa-opt-curve}
\end{figure}

The accepted-candidate scores are not monotone --- they run $0.766$, $0.736$, $0.795$, $0.711$, $0.762$, $0.657$ --- while the running best is. This follows from the Pareto rule of Section~\ref{sec:gepa}: an offspring that loses on the aggregate is still retained when it dominates the incumbent on individual instances, so the pool keeps candidates that a greedy search would discard. It also shows the search is not converging within this budget; the last accepted candidate is the worst of the six.

\section{What the episode does and does not establish}

The episode answers RQ3. The rule fired on a real, large, model-specific change, and the same-batch control rules out the shared-cause explanations. Over 265 comparisons it produced one such detection and one false alert traceable to a known weakness of the fixed-baseline design.

It does not answer RQ5. Answering RQ5 requires a degradation that persists long enough for prompt repair to be applied and its effect measured against the alternative of switching models. No such degradation occurred: the one real event lasted two days and recovered unaided, and the offline GEPA runs measure whether the mechanism can improve a prompt, not whether it can recover a drifted deployment. The two questions are separate, and only the first is settled here. Establishing the second needs a sustained episode, which four months of monitoring did not produce.
