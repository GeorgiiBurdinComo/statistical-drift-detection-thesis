\chapter{Data and Evaluation Setup}
\label{ch:data}

This chapter specifies the operational evaluation setting used throughout the thesis. It defines the classification problem, introduces the fixed labelled benchmark $\mathcal{D}_N$, explains how the frozen monitoring subset $\mathcal{D}_n$ with $n=300$ is constructed, gives a practical size rationale aligned with the McNemar procedure in Section~\ref{sec:mcnemar-section}, and clarifies what subset-based performance can and cannot be interpreted as. Repeated evaluation over time and the formal drift test are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:formalisation-temporal}.

\section{Formal problem definition}

At evaluation time $t$, the deployed API model is treated as a binary classifier
\[
f_t(x)=f(x;\pi,\theta_t)\in\{0,1\},
\]
where $\pi$ is the fixed prompt and $\theta_t$ denotes the provider-controlled model state at time $t$. For any benchmark item $x_i$, let
\[
\hat y_{i,t}=f_t(x_i)
\]
denote the predicted class label.

Ideally, one would like to evaluate the classifier on the real distribution of posts encountered at time $t$. Let $P_t$ denote that unobserved distribution. Under zero--one loss, the corresponding population risk is
\begin{equation}
    R_t(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P_t}\bigl[\mathbb{I}(f_t(x)\neq y)\bigr].
\end{equation}

In practice, $P_t$ is not observed. Evaluation therefore relies on a fixed labelled benchmark
\[
\mathcal{D}_N=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^N,\qquad N=1199.
\]
Because $\mathcal{D}_N$ is treated as a fixed reference population, the operational full-benchmark performance target at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    A_N(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}\bigl(\hat y_{i,t}=y_i\bigr).
\end{equation}

In general, $A_N(t)$ need not equal $1-R_t(f)$, because $\mathcal{D}_N$ is not assumed to be a probability sample from $P_t$. Accordingly, the thesis uses $A_N(t)$ and later subset-based analogues as operational evaluation quantities, while $R_t(f)$ serves only as the ideal population quantity motivating non-stationarity.

\section{Reference benchmark, operational constraint, and monitoring subset}
\label{sec:input-datasets}

Routine evaluation of every candidate model on the full benchmark is too expensive for high-frequency monitoring. In the implemented pipeline, one full pass over $\mathcal{D}_N$ costs about \$90, which is acceptable for initialization or explicit escalation, but not for weekly monitoring. The monitoring stage therefore uses a fixed subset
\[
\mathcal{D}_n\subset\mathcal{D}_N,\qquad n\ll N,
\]
with $n=300$.

The purpose of $\mathcal{D}_n$ is not to replace $\mathcal{D}_N$ as a representative benchmark. Its purpose is to provide a smaller, frozen panel on which repeated inference can be run cheaply enough to detect changes in behaviour over time.

The sets $\mathcal{D}_N$ and $\mathcal{D}_n$ are stored as fixed Langfuse datasets with a common schema and the binary label column \texttt{campaign\_relevant}. Table~\ref{tab:dataset-summary} summarises their composition.

The corresponding Langfuse dataset identifiers are \texttt{campaign\_relevance\_02e1a68ccb0f} for the full benchmark and \texttt{campaign\_relevance\_disagree\_subset\_9d488308aa46} for the monitoring subset.

Each post is classified as either an image post or a video post with multiple extracted frames. Both datasets are class-imbalanced, and the monitoring subset is substantially more positive-heavy than the full benchmark. This reflects the way $\mathcal{D}_n$ was constructed and immediately implies that accuracy values on $\mathcal{D}_N$ and on $\mathcal{D}_n$ are not comparable by magnitude alone.

Roughly three quarters of items are video posts in both sets. Since video posts contribute multiple frames, they typically incur higher multimodal token cost than single-image posts. The subset is therefore not a stratified copy of $\mathcal{D}_N$: it has a somewhat different class balance and a different difficulty mix. Later empirical monitoring results are reported on the fixed $\mathcal{D}_n$ unless explicitly stated otherwise.

\section{Disagreement-based subset construction}
\label{sec:informative-sampling-disagreement}

If the sole goal were unbiased estimation of the finite-population benchmark accuracy $A_N(t)$, one natural option would be to draw a simple random sample from $\mathcal{D}_N$. However, that is not the goal here. The monitoring panel is intended for repeated paired comparisons over time, where the main question is whether the deployed model has changed its behaviour on informative cases.

In the full benchmark, many items are comparatively easy: multiple models classify them the same way, and these cases are less likely to reveal subtle provider-side changes. For monitoring, such unanimously easy cases are less informative than cases on which candidate models disagree. The implemented design therefore enriches the monitoring subset with disagreement cases.

Let
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ens}}=\{f^{(1)},\dots,f^{(K)}\}
\end{equation}
denote the ensemble of $K$ models evaluated once on the full benchmark
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_N=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^N.
\end{equation}
For item $i$, let
\[
\hat y_i^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}
\]
be the prediction of model $f^{(k)}$.

The disagreement pool is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{dis}}
    =
    \left\{
        i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
        \;\middle|\;
        \exists\,k\neq \ell
        \text{ such that }
        \hat y_i^{(k)}\neq \hat y_i^{(\ell)}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}
Thus, an item belongs to $\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{dis}}$ if the ensemble does not unanimously agree on its label.

The monitoring subset is then obtained by selecting a fixed index set
\[
\mathcal{I}_n\subseteq \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{dis}},\qquad |\mathcal{I}_n|=n,
\]
and defining
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_n=\{(x_i,y_i):i\in\mathcal{I}_n\}\subset\mathcal{D}_N.
\end{equation}

The exact implementation-specific tie-breaking or ordering inside the disagreement pool is not essential for the methodological argument. What matters is the design principle: the monitoring panel is built from items on which the initial model ensemble does not fully agree, and once selected, that panel is frozen.

The rationale is operational. Disagreement cases are usually harder than unanimous cases, and harder cases are more informative for change detection. If a provider-side model update alters decision behaviour, it is more likely to become visible first on items that are already difficult or ambiguous for the ensemble. Monitoring such items therefore increases sensitivity to drift on a fixed budget.

This is a deliberate change of objective. The disagreement-based subset is not designed to be a minimum-variance estimator of full-benchmark accuracy. It is designed to be a drift-sensitive monitoring panel.

\section{Monitoring subset size}
\label{sec:monitoring-sample-size}

Section~\ref{sec:mcnemar-section} defines the discordant counts
\[
b=\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(E_{i,t_1}=1,E_{i,t_2}=0),
\qquad
c=\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(E_{i,t_1}=0,E_{i,t_2}=1),
\]
where $b$ counts regressions (correct $\to$ wrong) and $c$ counts improvements (wrong $\to$ correct). As shown in Section~\ref{sec:mcnemar-section},
\begin{equation}
    \hat A_{t_2}-\hat A_{t_1}=\frac{c-b}{n}.
\end{equation}

Hence a decrease in panel accuracy is driven by an excess of regressions over improvements. For planning, write
\begin{equation}
    p_b=P(E_{i,t_1}=1,E_{i,t_2}=0),
    \qquad
    p_c=P(E_{i,t_1}=0,E_{i,t_2}=1),
\end{equation}
under the monitoring distribution induced by $\mathcal{D}_n$, and define
\begin{equation}
    \rho=p_b+p_c
\end{equation}
as the discordance probability per item and
\begin{equation}
    \delta=p_b-p_c
\end{equation}
as the directional imbalance.

If the practically relevant event is a drop in panel accuracy of size $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}>0$, then
\[
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat A_{t_2}-\hat A_{t_1}\right]=p_c-p_b=-\delta,
\]
so the target directional effect is approximately
\[
\delta\approx \Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}.
\]

\paragraph{Conditional-binomial view of McNemar's test.}
McNemar's test depends only on the discordant pairs. Let
\[
m=b+c
\]
denote the number of discordant pairs. Conditional on $m$, the count $b$ of regressions is binomial:
\begin{equation}
    b\mid m \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(m,\eta),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \eta
    :=
    P(\text{regression}\mid \text{discordance})
    =
    \frac{p_b}{p_b+p_c}
    =
    \frac{p_b}{\rho}.
\end{equation}
Using
\[
p_b=\frac{\rho+\delta}{2},
\qquad
p_c=\frac{\rho-\delta}{2},
\]
gives
\begin{equation}
    \eta=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2\rho}.
\end{equation}

Under the no-drift null hypothesis, regressions and improvements are equally likely, so
\begin{equation}
    H_0:\eta=\frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}
For one-sided degradation detection, the alternative is
\begin{equation}
    H_a:\eta>\frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Normal-approximation planning rule.}
Conditional on $m$, the null distribution is
\[
b\mid m\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(m,1/2).
\]
For moderate or large $m$, this can be approximated by
\[
b\mid m \approx \mathcal N\!\left(\frac{m}{2},\frac{m}{4}\right).
\]
A one-sided level-$\alpha$ test rejects for sufficiently large $b$, namely when
\begin{equation}
    b \ge \frac{m}{2}+z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{m}{4}},
\end{equation}
where $z_{1-\alpha}$ denotes the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the standard normal distribution.

Under the alternative
\[
b\mid m\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(m,\eta),
\]
and the corresponding normal approximation is
\[
b\mid m \approx \mathcal N\!\bigl(m\eta,m\eta(1-\eta)\bigr).
\]
Requiring power $1-\beta$ leads to the sufficient condition
\begin{equation}
    m
    \ge
    \left(
        \frac{
            z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{0.25}+z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}
        }{
            \eta-0.5
        }
    \right)^2.
\end{equation}

Finally, since the expected number of discordant pairs is approximately
\[
m\approx n\rho,
\]
the corresponding panel-size requirement is
\begin{equation}
    n
    \ge
    \frac{1}{\rho}
    \left(
        \frac{
            z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{0.25}+z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}
        }{
            \eta-0.5
        }
    \right)^2,
    \qquad
    \eta=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2\rho}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Operational calibration.}
In this thesis, the minimum practically relevant degradation is set to
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}=0.05.
\end{equation}
Treating this as the target directional effect gives
\[
\delta\approx 0.05,
\qquad
\eta=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{0.05}{2\rho}.
\]

For the planning range $\rho\in[0.10,0.15]$, this yields
\[
\eta\in[0.667,0.75].
\]
For $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.05,0.2)$, the required discordant count is then approximately
\[
m\in[23,54],
\]
which corresponds to a total panel size of roughly
\[
n\in[225,357].
\]

Accordingly, the implemented choice
\[
n=300
\]
should be interpreted as a pragmatic mid-range design value. It is large enough to make paired monitoring informative at reasonable discordance rates, while keeping routine monitoring substantially cheaper than repeated full-benchmark evaluation. The calculation is therefore used as a planning heuristic, not as a sharp universal power guarantee.

\section{Selection bias and monitoring objective}
\label{sec:selection-bias}

Because the monitoring subset is drawn from disagreement cases, it is not a representative sample of the full benchmark. In particular, it over-represents hard cases relative to $\mathcal{D}_N$.

Define subset accuracy by
\begin{equation}
    \hat A_n(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_n}\mathbb{I}\bigl(\hat y_{i,t}=y_i\bigr).
\end{equation}
In general, $\hat A_n(t)$ is not an unbiased estimator of the full-benchmark accuracy $A_N(t)$.

This is intentional. The purpose of $\mathcal{D}_n$ is not to estimate $A_N(t)$ as accurately as possible from a smaller sample. Its purpose is to provide a stable, more sensitive panel for detecting behavioural change. The same property that makes the subset biased for full-benchmark estimation makes it useful for monitoring: difficult items are more likely than trivial items to expose changes in the model's decision behaviour.

Consequently, $\hat A_n(t)$ is interpreted as a monitoring statistic on a fixed hard-case panel, not as a direct surrogate for full-benchmark performance. This distinction is important for the later decision logic:

\begin{itemize}
    \item repeated paired drift detection is performed on the frozen subset $\mathcal{D}_n$;
    \item descriptive subset metrics are useful for identifying the current error profile on hard cases;
    \item final model comparison for deployment, especially when cost and class prevalence matter, must return to the full benchmark $\mathcal{D}_N$ or another representative evaluation set.
\end{itemize}

In short, the methodology separates two tasks that should not be conflated: \emph{monitoring for change} on a drift-sensitive panel and \emph{estimating deployment quality} on the full benchmark.
