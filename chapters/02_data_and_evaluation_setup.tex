\chapter{Data and Evaluation Setup}
\label{ch:data}

This chapter defines the evaluation setting used in the thesis. It introduces the labelled benchmark $\mathcal{D}_N$, the fixed monitoring subset $\mathcal{D}_n$ with $n=300$, and a planning-based rationale for its size. It also clarifies how performance on $\mathcal{D}_n$ should be interpreted. Repeated evaluation over time and the formal drift test are presented later in Section~\ref{sec:formalisation-temporal}.

\section{Formal problem definition}

At time $t$, the deployed API model is treated as a binary classifier
\[
f_t(x)=f(x;\pi,\theta_t)\in\{0,1\},
\]
where $\pi$ is the fixed prompt and $\theta_t$ is the provider-controlled model state at time $t$. For benchmark item $x_i$, let
\[
\hat y_{i,t}=f_t(x_i)
\]
denote the predicted label.

Ideally, performance would be measured on the true data distribution at time $t$. Let $P_t$ denote this unobserved distribution. Under zero--one loss, the corresponding population risk is
\begin{equation}
    R_t(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P_t}\bigl[\mathbb{I}(f_t(x)\neq y)\bigr].
\end{equation}

In practice, $P_t$ is not observed. Evaluation therefore uses a fixed labelled benchmark
\[
\mathcal{D}_N=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^N,\qquad N=1199.
\]
The set $\mathcal{D}_N$ was not created by the monitoring method itself. It already existed before this study as a manually labelled internal benchmark for the campaign-relevance task and is treated here as a fixed input dataset.

The full-benchmark accuracy at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    A_N(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}\bigl(\hat y_{i,t}=y_i\bigr).
\end{equation}

In general, $A_N(t)$ does not have to equal $1-R_t(f)$, because $\mathcal{D}_N$ is not assumed to be a probability sample from $P_t$. Therefore, the thesis uses $A_N(t)$ and corresponding subset-based quantities as operational evaluation targets, while $R_t(f)$ only motivates the non-stationary setting.

\section{Reference benchmark, operational constraint, and monitoring subset}
\label{sec:input-datasets}

Evaluating every candidate model on the full benchmark at high frequency is too expensive for routine monitoring. In the implemented pipeline, one full run over $\mathcal{D}_N$ costs about \$90. This is acceptable for setup or re-evaluation, but not for weekly monitoring.

For this reason, monitoring is performed on a fixed subset
\[
\mathcal{D}_n\subset\mathcal{D}_N,\qquad n\ll N,
\]
with $n=300$.

The role of $\mathcal{D}_n$ is not to replace $\mathcal{D}_N$. It is a smaller frozen panel for repeated inference over time.

The datasets $\mathcal{D}_N$ and $\mathcal{D}_n$ are stored in Langfuse with the same schema and the binary label column \texttt{campaign\_relevant}. Table~\ref{tab:dataset-summary} summarises their composition.

Each post is either an image post or a video post with multiple extracted frames. Both datasets are class-imbalanced. The subset $\mathcal{D}_n$ is much more positive-heavy than $\mathcal{D}_N$, which follows from its construction. Therefore, accuracy values on $\mathcal{D}_N$ and on $\mathcal{D}_n$ are not directly comparable.

The modality mix also differs slightly. In both datasets, most posts are videos. Since video posts contain multiple frames, they are usually more expensive to evaluate than single-image posts. Thus, $\mathcal{D}_n$ is not a stratified copy of $\mathcal{D}_N$.

\section{Disagreement-based subset construction}
\label{sec:informative-sampling-disagreement}

A simple random sample from $\mathcal{D}_N$ would be suitable if the main goal were unbiased estimation of $A_N(t)$. This is not the goal here. The subset is used for repeated paired monitoring, where the main goal is to detect changes in model behaviour.

Many items in the full benchmark are easy: several models classify them in the same way. Such cases are less informative for monitoring than cases on which models disagree. Therefore, the monitoring subset is constructed from disagreement cases.

Let
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ens}}=\{f^{(1)},\dots,f^{(K)}\}
\end{equation}
be the ensemble of $K$ models evaluated on the full benchmark
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_N=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^N.
\end{equation}
For item $i$, let
\[
\hat y_i^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}
\]
denote the prediction of model $f^{(k)}$.

Define the disagreement index set
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{dis}}
    =
    \left\{
        i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
        \;\middle|\;
        \exists\,k\neq \ell
        \text{ such that }
        \hat y_i^{(k)}\neq \hat y_i^{(\ell)}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}
Thus, an item belongs to $\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{dis}}$ if the ensemble does not fully agree on its label.

The monitoring subset is then defined by a fixed index set
\[
\mathcal{I}_n\subseteq \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{dis}},\qquad |\mathcal{I}_n|=n,
\]
and
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_n=\{(x_i,y_i):i\in\mathcal{I}_n\}\subset\mathcal{D}_N.
\end{equation}

The reason is simple. Disagreement cases are usually harder than unanimous cases. Harder cases are more likely to reveal small changes in decision behaviour. Therefore, they are more useful for drift monitoring under a limited budget.

\section{Monitoring subset size}
\label{sec:monitoring-sample-size}

For item $i$ at time $t$, define the correctness indicator
\begin{equation}
    E_{i,t}=\mathbb{I}(\hat y_{i,t}=y_i).
\end{equation}

Section~\ref{sec:mcnemar-section} defines the discordant counts
\[
b=\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(E_{i,t_1}=1,E_{i,t_2}=0),
\qquad
c=\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(E_{i,t_1}=0,E_{i,t_2}=1),
\]
where $b$ counts regressions (correct $\to$ wrong) and $c$ counts improvements (wrong $\to$ correct). As shown in Section~\ref{sec:mcnemar-section},
\begin{equation}
    \hat A_{t_2}-\hat A_{t_1}=\frac{c-b}{n}.
\end{equation}

Thus, a decrease in panel accuracy implies $b>c$, i.e.\ regressions outnumber improvements.

For planning, let $P(\cdot)$ denote probability for a generic item under the monitoring model, and let $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ denote expectation with respect to the same probability law. Define
\begin{equation}
    p_b=P(E_{i,t_1}=1,E_{i,t_2}=0),
    \qquad
    p_c=P(E_{i,t_1}=0,E_{i,t_2}=1).
\end{equation}
Here, $p_b$ is the probability that an item changes from correct to wrong between $t_1$ and $t_2$, and $p_c$ is the probability that it changes from wrong to correct.

Let
\begin{equation}
    \rho=p_b+p_c
\end{equation}
be the discordance probability per item and
\begin{equation}
    \delta=p_b-p_c
\end{equation}
the directional imbalance.

If the practically relevant event is a drop in panel accuracy of size $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}>0$, then
\[
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat A_{t_2}-\hat A_{t_1}\right]=p_c-p_b=-\delta,
\]
so the target effect is approximated by
\[
\delta\approx \Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}.
\]

\paragraph{Conditional view of McNemar's test.}
For each item, the paired correctness outcome $(E_{i,t_1},E_{i,t_2})$ can fall into one of four categories:
\[
(1,1),\quad (1,0),\quad (0,1),\quad (0,0),
\]
with probabilities
\[
p_a=P(1,1),\quad
p_b=P(1,0),\quad
p_c=P(0,1),\quad
p_d=P(0,0),
\]
respectively. These probabilities satisfy
\[
p_a+p_b+p_c+p_d=1.
\]

Assume that the $n$ paired items are independent and identically distributed across these four categories. Then the count vector $(a,b,c,d)$ follows a multinomial distribution:
\begin{equation}
    (a,b,c,d)\sim \mathrm{Multinomial}(n;p_a,p_b,p_c,p_d).
\end{equation}

McNemar's test uses only the discordant counts $b$ and $c$. Let
\[
m=b+c.
\]
To derive the conditional law of $b$ given $m$, fix an integer $k\in\{0,\dots,m\}$. Then
\begin{equation}
    P(b=k\mid b+c=m)=\frac{P(b=k,\;c=m-k)}{P(b+c=m)}.
\end{equation}

From the multinomial model,
\begin{equation}
    P(b=k,\;c=m-k)
    =
    \frac{n!}{k!\,(m-k)!\,(n-m)!}
    (p_a+p_d)^{n-m}p_b^k p_c^{m-k}.
\end{equation}
Also,
\begin{equation}
    P(b+c=m)
    =
    \frac{n!}{m!\,(n-m)!}
    (p_a+p_d)^{n-m}(p_b+p_c)^m.
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{align}
P(b=k\mid b+c=m)
&=
\frac{
\frac{n!}{k!\,(m-k)!\,(n-m)!}
(p_a+p_d)^{n-m}p_b^k p_c^{m-k}
}{
\frac{n!}{m!\,(n-m)!}
(p_a+p_d)^{n-m}(p_b+p_c)^m
} \\
&=
\frac{m!}{k!\,(m-k)!}
\left(\frac{p_b}{p_b+p_c}\right)^k
\left(\frac{p_c}{p_b+p_c}\right)^{m-k}.
\end{align}

This is the probability mass function of a binomial distribution. Hence
\begin{equation}
    b\mid (b+c=m)\sim \mathrm{Binomial}\!\left(m,\frac{p_b}{p_b+p_c}\right).
\end{equation}

Define
\begin{equation}
    \eta=\frac{p_b}{p_b+p_c}=\frac{p_b}{\rho}.
\end{equation}
Since
\[
p_b=\frac{\rho+\delta}{2},
\qquad
p_c=\frac{\rho-\delta}{2},
\]
it follows that
\begin{equation}
    \eta=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2\rho}.
\end{equation}

Under the null hypothesis of no directional change,
\begin{equation}
    H_0:p_b=p_c,
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}
    \eta=\frac12
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
    b\mid (b+c=m)\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(m,1/2).
\end{equation}

This means that, under $H_0$, once the total number of discordant items $m$ is fixed, each discordant item is equally likely to be a regression or an improvement. This conditional binomial law is the basis of the exact McNemar test.

For one-sided degradation detection, the alternative is
\begin{equation}
    H_a:\eta>\frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{From required discordant pairs to subset size.}
The sample-size argument first determines how many discordant pairs are needed for the McNemar test, and only then converts this number into a subset size. The normal approximation in this subsection is used only for planning; the actual drift test in Section~\ref{sec:mcnemar-section} uses the exact conditional McNemar test.

Under $H_0$,
\[
b\mid m\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(m,1/2).
\]
For moderate or large $m$, the binomial distribution can be approximated by a normal distribution via the Central Limit Theorem. Since
\[
b\mid m \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(m,1/2),
\]
it can be written as a sum of $m$ independent Bernoulli variables. Therefore,
\[
\mathbb{E}[b\mid m]=\frac{m}{2},
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(b\mid m)=\frac{m}{4},
\]
and for sufficiently large $m$,
\begin{equation}
    b\mid m \approx \mathcal N\!\left(\frac{m}{2},\frac{m}{4}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ denotes a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$.

A common rule of thumb for the normal approximation to a $\mathrm{Binomial}(m,q)$ distribution is
\[
mq\ge 5
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
m(1-q)\ge 5.
\]
Here $q=1/2$, so this requires $m\ge 10$. The estimated values of $m$ in this work are well above this threshold.

Let $z_{1-\alpha}$ and $z_{1-\beta}$ denote the $(1-\alpha)$- and $(1-\beta)$-quantiles of the standard normal distribution. A one-sided level-$\alpha$ test rejects for large $b$, that is,
\begin{equation}
    b \ge \frac{m}{2}+z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{m}{4}}.
\end{equation}

Under the alternative,
\[
b\mid m\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(m,\eta),
\qquad
b\mid m \approx \mathcal N\!\bigl(m\eta,m\eta(1-\eta)\bigr).
\]
To obtain power at least $1-\beta$, require
\begin{equation}
    P_\eta\!\left(
        b \ge \frac{m}{2}+z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{m}{4}}
    \right)\ge 1-\beta.
\end{equation}
After standardization, a sufficient condition is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{
        m(\eta-\tfrac12)-z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{m/4}
    }{
        \sqrt{m\eta(1-\eta)}
    }
    \ge z_{1-\beta}.
\end{equation}
Rearranging gives
\begin{equation}
    m
    \ge
    \left(
        \frac{
            z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{0.25}+z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}
        }{
            \eta-0.5
        }
    \right)^2.
    \label{eq:planning-discordant-m}
\end{equation}

This gives the required number of discordant pairs. To convert it into a panel size, use
\[
m\approx n\rho,
\]
because $\rho$ is the probability that a panel item is discordant. Hence the required subset size is approximately
\begin{equation}
    n
    \ge
    \frac{1}{\rho}
    \left(
        \frac{
            z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{0.25}+z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}
        }{
            \eta-0.5
        }
    \right)^2,
    \qquad
    \eta=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2\rho}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Operational calibration.}
First, the discordance probability was estimated from a pilot repeated evaluation of the production model on the full benchmark $\mathcal{D}_N$. Running the same 1199-item dataset twice produced 137 discordant items. Hence the empirical discordance rate is
\begin{equation}
    \hat\rho=\frac{137}{1199}\approx 0.1143.
\end{equation}

Second, the minimum relevant degradation was set to
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}=0.05.
\end{equation}
This value was chosen as an operational threshold motivated by internal annotation practice: especially difficult or disputed posts were on the order of a few percent and were typically reviewed jointly by several analysts. Since this threshold was part of internal workflow practice rather than a formal company-wide metric, it is used here as a pragmatic design assumption.

With
\[
\delta\approx \Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}=0.05,
\qquad
\hat\rho\approx 0.1143,
\]
one obtains
\begin{equation}
    \eta=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2\rho}
    \approx
    \frac{1}{2}+\frac{0.05}{2\cdot 0.1143}
    \approx 0.7187.
\end{equation}

For $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.05,0.2)$, this gives a required discordant count of approximately
\begin{equation}
    m\approx 31.1.
\end{equation}
Using $m\approx n\rho$, the corresponding estimated subset size is
\begin{equation}
    n\approx \frac{31.1}{0.1143}\approx 272.2.
\end{equation}
Rounding up gives an estimated planning size of about
\[
n\approx 273.
\]

The implemented choice
\[
n=300
\]
is therefore slightly larger than the planning minimum under these assumptions. It should be interpreted as a conservative practical rounding up.

\section{Selection bias and monitoring objective}
\label{sec:selection-bias}

Because the monitoring subset is built from disagreement cases, it is not representative of the full benchmark. In particular, it over-represents hard cases.

Define subset accuracy by
\begin{equation}
    \hat A_n(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_n}\mathbb{I}\bigl(\hat y_{i,t}=y_i\bigr).
\end{equation}
In general, $\hat A_n(t)$ is not an unbiased estimator of $A_N(t)$.

This is intentional. The subset is not designed for accurate estimation of full-benchmark accuracy, but for sensitive monitoring. Hard cases are more likely to change under small model updates, so they are more useful for drift detection.

Therefore, $\hat A_n(t)$ is interpreted as a monitoring statistic on a fixed hard-case panel, not as a replacement for full-benchmark performance. The methodology keeps these two tasks separate:
\begin{itemize}
    \item drift detection is performed on the fixed subset $\mathcal{D}_n$;
    \item final model comparison for deployment returns to the full benchmark $\mathcal{D}_N$.
\end{itemize}
