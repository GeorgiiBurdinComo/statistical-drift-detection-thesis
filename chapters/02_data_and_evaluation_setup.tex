\chapter{Data and Evaluation Setup}
\label{ch:data}

The chapter specifies the classification task, the reference benchmark, construction of the fixed monitoring subset $\mathcal{D}_n$ (with $n=300$), a size rationale aligned with the McNemar procedure in Section~\ref{sec:mcnemar-section}, and the interpretation of subset-based accuracy relative to the full benchmark. Repeated evaluation over time and the operational drift test are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:formalisation-temporal}.

\section{Formal problem definition}

Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote the space of social-media posts, including textual content, metadata, and attached visual content, and let $\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}$ denote the binary label space ($1$: campaign-relevant).. Under LLM-as-a-Service, the deployed classifier is a non-stationary mapping $f(x;\pi,\theta_t)\mapsto \hat{y}\in\{0,1\}$ with fixed prompt $\pi$ and provider-controlled weights $\theta_t$.

Under zero--one loss $\mathcal{L}(\hat{y},y)=\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}\neq y)$, the population risk at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    R_t(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P_t}\bigl[\mathbb{I}\bigl(f(x;\pi,\theta_t)\neq y\bigr)\bigr],
\end{equation}
for a distribution $P_t$. The distribution $P_t$ is not observed.

The study uses a fixed labelled benchmark $\mathcal{D}_N=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ with $N=1199$, treated as the reference population for offline evaluation. For any fixed $t$, the benchmark accuracy
\begin{equation}
    A_N(t) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}\bigl(f(x_i;\pi,\theta_t)=y_i\bigr)
\end{equation}
is the sample accuracy on the benchmark $\mathcal{D}_N$. It is the estimand for full-benchmark runs. Unless $\mathcal{D}_N$ is a probability sample from $P_t$, $A_N(t)$ need not equal $1-R_t(f)$; the two objects are not identified from each other without additional assumptions. Subsequent sections use $A_N$ and subset accuracy as operational targets; $R_t$ motivates non-stationarity of $f(\cdot;\pi,\theta_t)$.

\section{Reference benchmark, operational constraint, and monitoring subset}
\label{sec:input-datasets}

Full-benchmark evaluation of every candidate at high frequency is prohibitively expensive (about \$90 per full run), which is too much for routine monitoring. Monitoring uses a fixed subset $\mathcal{D}_n\subset\mathcal{D}_N$ with $n\ll N$ on which the pipeline repeats inference; here $n=300$. The design goal is sensitivity to changes in $\theta_t$, not unbiased estimation of $A_N(t)$ from $\mathcal{D}_n$ alone.

The sets $\mathcal{D}_N$ and $\mathcal{D}_n$ are stored as fixed Langfuse datasets with common schema and label column (\texttt{campaign\_relevant}). Table~\ref{tab:dataset-summary} summarises composition.

\section{Disagreement-based subset construction}
\label{sec:informative-sampling-disagreement}

A simple random sample (SRS) of size $n$ from $\mathcal{D}_N$ yields an unbiased estimator of the benchmark accuracy $A_N(t)$ on $\mathcal{D}_N$ viewed as a finite population (simple random sampling without replacement). It does not, without further structure, target $R_t(f)$ under unknown $P_t$. Most benchmark items are easy for a given model ensemble; they show little cross-model variance and are therefore unlikely to change classification under small provider-side shifts in $\theta_t$.

To increase sensitivity to such changes, the monitoring subset is constructed by ensemble disagreement sampling: priority is given to benchmark items on which several distinct models do not agree.

Let
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ens}}=\{f^{(1)},\dots,f^{(K)}\}
\end{equation}
denote an ensemble of $K$ classifiers evaluated on the full benchmark
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_N=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^N.
\end{equation}
Here:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $N$ is the size of the full benchmark;
    \item $i\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ indexes benchmark items;
    \item $x_i$ is the input associated with item $i$;
    \item $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ is the reference label of item $i$;
    \item $K$ is the number of models in the ensemble;
    \item $f^{(k)}$ is the $k$-th ensemble model, for $k\in\{1,\dots,K\}$;
    \item $\hat y_i^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}$ is the prediction of model $f^{(k)}$ on item $i$.
\end{itemize}

Define the disagreement pool
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{dis}}
    =
    \left\{
        x_i \in \mathcal{D}_N
        \;\middle|\;
        \exists\, k,\ell \in \{1,\dots,K\}
        \text{ such that }
        \hat y_i^{(k)} \neq \hat y_i^{(\ell)}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}
The monitoring subset $\mathcal{D}_n$ is then obtained by applying a fixed selection rule to $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{dis}}$ so that $|\mathcal{D}_n|=n$.

The objective of this construction is not minimum-variance estimation of $A_N(t)$ on the full benchmark. Its objective is drift sensitivity: the subset should contain items whose predicted label is comparatively unstable and therefore more likely to react to provider-side model updates.

\subsection{Latent-margin model for disagreement sampling}

The deployed LLM classifier is treated as a black box: only binary outputs are observed, not internal representations, logits, or calibrated probabilities. To formalise disagreement-based sampling introduce a latent-margin model at the level of benchmark items.

\paragraph{Notation and interpretation.}
For each benchmark item $i\in\{1,\dots,N\}$, let
\begin{equation}
    m_i \in \mathbb{R}
\end{equation}
denote an unobserved signed relevance margin. The sign of $m_i$ determines the reference class, and the magnitude $|m_i|$ measures how far the item is from the relevance threshold:

\begin{itemize}
    \item if $m_i>0$, the item is relevant;
    \item if $m_i<0$, the item is not relevant;
    \item if $|m_i|$ is large, the item is unambiguous;
    \item if $m_i$ is close to $0$, the item is borderline.
\end{itemize}

The reference label is therefore written as
\begin{equation}
    y_i=\mathbb{I}(m_i\ge 0),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function, equal to $1$ when its argument is true and $0$ otherwise.

Next, let $u$ denote an evaluator. An evaluator can be either:
\begin{itemize}
    \item one of the ensemble models used during subset construction, or
    \item the deployed provider model at a particular monitoring time.
\end{itemize}

For evaluator $u$, the observed prediction on item $i$ is modelled as
\begin{equation}
    \hat y_i^{(u)}
    =
    \mathbb{I}\!\left(m_i+\varepsilon_i^{(u)}\ge 0\right),
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_i^{(u)}$ is a random noise term. Intuitively, $\varepsilon_i^{(u)}$ represents evaluator-specific perturbation around the latent item margin $m_i$.

Assume that, conditional on $m_i$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the noise variables $\varepsilon_i^{(u)}$ are independent and identically distributed across evaluators;
    \item they have a continuous cumulative distribution function (CDF)
    \begin{equation}
        G(z)=P(\varepsilon_i^{(u)}\le z);
    \end{equation}
    \item the distribution is symmetric around $0$, so that
    \begin{equation}
        G(-z)=1-G(z)
        \qquad \text{for all } z\in\mathbb{R}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

The symmetry assumption means that positive and negative perturbations of the same magnitude are equally likely.

\paragraph{Probability of a positive prediction.}
Define
\begin{equation}
    q_i
    :=
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=1 \mid m_i\right).
\end{equation}
This is the conditional probability that evaluator $u$ predicts class $1$ on item $i$, given the latent margin $m_i$.

We now compute $q_i$ explicitly:
\begin{align}
    q_i
    &= P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=1 \mid m_i\right) \\
    &= P\!\left(m_i+\varepsilon_i^{(u)}\ge 0 \mid m_i\right) \\
    &= P\!\left(\varepsilon_i^{(u)}\ge -m_i \mid m_i\right) \\
    &= 1 - P\!\left(\varepsilon_i^{(u)} < -m_i \mid m_i\right).
\end{align}
Because the noise distribution is continuous, strict and non-strict inequalities have the same probability, so
\begin{equation}
    P\!\left(\varepsilon_i^{(u)} < -m_i \mid m_i\right)
    =
    P\!\left(\varepsilon_i^{(u)} \le -m_i \mid m_i\right)
    =
    G(-m_i).
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
    q_i = 1-G(-m_i).
\end{equation}
Using symmetry, $G(-m_i)=1-G(m_i)$, so
\begin{equation}
    q_i = G(m_i).
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Pairwise disagreement is largest for borderline items]
\label{prop:pairwise-disagreement-margin}
Let $u$ and $v$ be two evaluators. Assume that, conditional on $m_i$, their noise terms are independent. Define
\begin{equation}
    D_i
    :=
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}\neq \hat y_i^{(v)} \mid m_i\right).
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
    D_i = 2G(m_i)\bigl(1-G(m_i)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Moreover, $D_i$ is maximal at $m_i=0$ and non-increasing in $|m_i|$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix an item $i$ and condition on its latent margin $m_i$.

The event
\begin{equation}
    \{\hat y_i^{(u)}\neq \hat y_i^{(v)}\}
\end{equation}
can happen in exactly two mutually exclusive ways:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item evaluator $u$ predicts $1$ and evaluator $v$ predicts $0$;
    \item evaluator $u$ predicts $0$ and evaluator $v$ predicts $1$.
\end{enumerate}
Therefore,
\begin{align}
    D_i
    &=
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=1,\hat y_i^{(v)}=0 \mid m_i\right)
    +
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=0,\hat y_i^{(v)}=1 \mid m_i\right).
\end{align}

By conditional independence,
\begin{align}
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=1,\hat y_i^{(v)}=0 \mid m_i\right)
    &=
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=1 \mid m_i\right)
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(v)}=0 \mid m_i\right) \\
    &= q_i(1-q_i),
\end{align}
and similarly,
\begin{align}
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=0,\hat y_i^{(v)}=1 \mid m_i\right)
    &=
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=0 \mid m_i\right)
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(v)}=1 \mid m_i\right) \\
    &= (1-q_i)q_i.
\end{align}
Adding the two terms gives
\begin{equation}
    D_i = q_i(1-q_i) + (1-q_i)q_i = 2q_i(1-q_i).
\end{equation}
Since $q_i=G(m_i)$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
    D_i = 2G(m_i)\bigl(1-G(m_i)\bigr).
\end{equation}

It remains to show where this expression is largest. Consider the function
\begin{equation}
    h(p)=2p(1-p), \qquad p\in[0,1].
\end{equation}
This is a quadratic function with derivative
\begin{equation}
    h'(p)=2-4p.
\end{equation}
Hence:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $h'(p)>0$ for $p<1/2$;
    \item $h'(p)=0$ at $p=1/2$;
    \item $h'(p)<0$ for $p>1/2$.
\end{itemize}
So $h(p)$ is maximised at $p=1/2$.

Now, by symmetry of $G$, we have
\begin{equation}
    G(0)=\frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}
Therefore $D_i=h(G(m_i))$ is maximised when $G(m_i)=1/2$, that is, when $m_i=0$.

Finally, to show monotonicity in $|m_i|$, note first that
\begin{equation}
    D_i(-m_i)
    =
    2G(-m_i)\bigl(1-G(-m_i)\bigr)
    =
    2(1-G(m_i))G(m_i)
    =
    D_i(m_i).
\end{equation}
So $D_i$ is symmetric around $0$.

For $m_i>0$, the CDF $G$ is non-decreasing, so $G(m_i)\ge 1/2$ and increases with $m_i$. On the interval $[1/2,1]$, the function $h(p)=2p(1-p)$ is non-increasing. Therefore $D_i=h(G(m_i))$ is non-increasing for $m_i>0$. By symmetry, the same is true as $|m_i|$ increases on the negative side. Hence $D_i$ is non-increasing in $|m_i|$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Correctness probability.}
Define the correctness indicator
\begin{equation}
    E_i^{(u)}=\mathbb{I}\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=y_i\right).
\end{equation}
This variable equals $1$ if evaluator $u$ classifies item $i$ correctly, and $0$ otherwise.

Next define
\begin{equation}
    c_i
    :=
    P\!\left(E_i^{(u)}=1\mid m_i\right),
\end{equation}
the conditional probability that evaluator $u$ is correct on item $i$.

We now compute $c_i$ by considering the two cases $m_i\ge 0$ and $m_i<0$.

If $m_i\ge 0$, then $y_i=1$, so correctness means predicting class $1$. Hence
\begin{equation}
    c_i
    =
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=1\mid m_i\right)
    =
    G(m_i).
\end{equation}

If $m_i<0$, then $y_i=0$, so correctness means predicting class $0$. Hence
\begin{align}
    c_i
    &=
    P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=0\mid m_i\right) \\
    &=
    1-P\!\left(\hat y_i^{(u)}=1\mid m_i\right) \\
    &=
    1-G(m_i).
\end{align}
Using symmetry, $1-G(m_i)=G(-m_i)=G(|m_i|)$ because now $|m_i|=-m_i$. Thus in both cases,
\begin{equation}
    c_i = G(|m_i|).
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Temporal correctness flips are largest for borderline items]
\label{prop:correctness-flips-margin}
Let $t_1<t_2$ be two monitoring times. Assume that, conditional on $m_i$, the evaluator noises at times $t_1$ and $t_2$ are independent. Then
\begin{equation}
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_1)}\neq E_i^{(t_2)} \mid m_i\right)
    =
    2G(|m_i|)\bigl(1-G(|m_i|)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Moreover, this probability is maximal at $m_i=0$ and non-increasing in $|m_i|$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix an item $i$ and condition on $m_i$.

The event
\begin{equation}
    \{E_i^{(t_1)}\neq E_i^{(t_2)}\}
\end{equation}
means that the classifier is correct at one monitoring time and incorrect at the other. This can happen in exactly two mutually exclusive ways:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the classifier is correct at time $t_1$ and incorrect at time $t_2$;
    \item the classifier is incorrect at time $t_1$ and correct at time $t_2$.
\end{enumerate}
Therefore,
\begin{align}
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_1)}\neq E_i^{(t_2)} \mid m_i\right)
    &=
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_1)}=1,E_i^{(t_2)}=0 \mid m_i\right) \\
    &\quad +
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_1)}=0,E_i^{(t_2)}=1 \mid m_i\right).
\end{align}

By conditional independence and by the definition of $c_i$,
\begin{align}
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_1)}=1,E_i^{(t_2)}=0 \mid m_i\right)
    &=
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_1)}=1 \mid m_i\right)
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_2)}=0 \mid m_i\right) \\
    &=
    c_i(1-c_i),
\end{align}
and similarly,
\begin{align}
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_1)}=0,E_i^{(t_2)}=1 \mid m_i\right)
    &=
    (1-c_i)c_i.
\end{align}
Adding the two terms yields
\begin{equation}
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_1)}\neq E_i^{(t_2)} \mid m_i\right)
    =
    2c_i(1-c_i).
\end{equation}
Since $c_i=G(|m_i|)$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
    P\!\left(E_i^{(t_1)}\neq E_i^{(t_2)} \mid m_i\right)
    =
    2G(|m_i|)\bigl(1-G(|m_i|)\bigr).
\end{equation}

The remaining statement follows from the same argument as in Proposition~\ref{prop:pairwise-disagreement-margin}: the function $h(p)=2p(1-p)$ is maximised at $p=1/2$ and decreases as $p$ moves away from $1/2$. Since $G(|m_i|)\ge 1/2$ and increases with $|m_i|$, the flip probability is maximal at $|m_i|=0$, i.e.\ at $m_i=0$, and is non-increasing in $|m_i|$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Empirical disagreement score.}
For an ensemble of size $K$, define
\begin{equation}
    r_i
    =
    \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \mathbb{I}\!\left(\hat y_i^{(k)}=1\right).
\end{equation}
The quantity $r_i$ is the fraction of ensemble models predicting class $1$ on item $i$.

Now define the empirical disagreement score
\begin{equation}
    d_i = 4r_i(1-r_i).
\end{equation}
This score satisfies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $d_i=0$ when all models agree, because then $r_i\in\{0,1\}$;
    \item $d_i$ is largest when the ensemble is evenly split, that is, when $r_i\approx 1/2$.
\end{itemize}

Under the latent-margin model, conditional on $m_i$, the random variables
\begin{equation}
    Z_{ik}:=\mathbb{I}\!\left(\hat y_i^{(k)}=1\right),
    \qquad k=1,\dots,K,
\end{equation}
are i.i.d.\ Bernoulli with success probability $q_i=G(m_i)$. Since
\begin{equation}
    r_i=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K Z_{ik},
\end{equation}
we have
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[r_i\mid m_i]=q_i
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Var}(r_i\mid m_i)=\frac{q_i(1-q_i)}{K}.
\end{equation}

Using the identity
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[r_i(1-r_i)\mid m_i]
    =
    \mathbb{E}[r_i\mid m_i]
    -
    \mathbb{E}[r_i^2\mid m_i],
\end{equation}
together with
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[r_i^2\mid m_i]
    =
    \mathrm{Var}(r_i\mid m_i)
    +
    \bigl(\mathbb{E}[r_i\mid m_i]\bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
we obtain
\begin{align}
    \mathbb{E}[r_i(1-r_i)\mid m_i]
    &=
    q_i-\left(\frac{q_i(1-q_i)}{K}+q_i^2\right) \\
    &=
    q_i(1-q_i)\left(1-\frac{1}{K}\right) \\
    &=
    \frac{K-1}{K}q_i(1-q_i).
\end{align}
Multiplying by $4$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[d_i\mid m_i]
    =
    4\frac{K-1}{K}q_i(1-q_i)
    =
    4\frac{K-1}{K}G(m_i)\bigl(1-G(m_i)\bigr).
\end{equation}

Thus the observable score $d_i$ is a finite-$K$ proxy for latent instability: in expectation, it is largest for borderline items with $m_i$ close to $0$.

This reduced-form model does not claim to describe the internal mechanism of the LLM. Its role is operational: it explains, why disagreement-enriched subsets concentrate items with higher expected temporal flip probability, which in turn increases the expected number of discordant correctness pairs for paired drift tests such as McNemar's test.
\section{Monitoring subset size}
\label{sec:monitoring-sample-size}

Section~\ref{sec:mcnemar-section} defines discordant counts $b$ (regressions) and $c$ (improvements) between $t_1$ and $t_2$ and the McNemar statistic. For design, let $p_b=P(E_{i,t_1}=1,E_{i,t_2}=0)$ and $p_c=P(E_{i,t_1}=0,E_{i,t_2}=1)$ under the monitoring distribution on $\mathcal{D}_n$. Write $\rho=p_b+p_c$ for the discordance probability per item and $\delta=p_b-p_c$ for directional imbalance. On $\mathcal{D}_n$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{A}_{t_2}-\hat{A}_{t_1}=\frac{c-b}{n}
\end{equation}
(see Section~\ref{sec:mcnemar-section}). Detecting a benchmark-relevant drop in accuracy of at least $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}=0.05$ on the panel is related to $\delta$ and $\rho$; McNemar's power rises when $\rho$ is larger (more discordant pairs for fixed $n$).

The choice $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}=0.05$ is tied to the minimum detectable effect relative to label noise. In production annotation, human inter-annotator disagreement on borderline posts induces an irreducible error rate on the order of $\epsilon_{\mathrm{noise}}\approx 0.05$; drifts smaller than that are not reliably distinguishable from background labelling variability. The planning calculation below therefore treats $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}=0.05$ as an operational margin aligned with that floor, not as a universal statistical identity.

A normal-approximation sample-size requirement for a one-sided level-$\alpha$ test with power $1-\beta$ takes the form
\begin{equation}
    n \ge \frac{1}{\rho} \left(
        \frac{
            z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{0.25} + z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{\pi(1-\pi)}
        }{
            \pi-0.5
        }
    \right)^2,
    \qquad
    \pi = \frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2\rho},
\end{equation}
with $\pi$ the conditional probability of a regression given discordance. Plausible planning ranges $\rho\in[0.10,0.15]$ on $\mathcal{D}_n$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}=0.05$ imply $\pi\in[0.667,0.75]$. For $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.05,0.2)$, the implied discordant count $m=n\rho$ is about $23$--$29$, hence $n\approx m/\rho\in[190,290]$. Setting $n=300$ is a conservative operational choice yielding roughly $30$--$45$ discordant pairs when $\rho$ lies in that range, sufficient for one-sided McNemar-style alerts at $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}\approx 0.05$ under the stated regime. This is a planning value, not a universal lower bound.

\section{Selection bias and importance weighting}
\label{sec:selection-bias}

Because $\mathcal{D}_n$ oversamples high-$d_i$ instances, the raw subset accuracy
\begin{equation}
    \hat{A}_n(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_n}\mathbb{I}\bigl(\hat{y}_{i,t}=y_i\bigr)
\end{equation}
is generally biased for $A_N(t)$: the estimands differ. The design accepts that bias to amplify instability where ensemble disagreement is large. Temporal shifts in provider weights $\theta_t$ are expected to surface first on such items, improving power of the paired monitor relative to a uniform $\mathcal{D}_n$.

Relating $\hat{A}_n$ to $A_N$ by inverse probability weighting requires a sampling design with known inclusion probabilities $q_i=P(i\in\mathcal{D}_n)>0$ for all $i\in\mathcal{D}_N$. The implemented rule is deterministic disagreement-based selection with $q_i=0$ for many $i$; Horvitz--Thompson (HT) recovery of $A_N$ from $\mathcal{D}_n$ alone is therefore not defined.

\subsection{Horvitz--Thompson estimator under a positive design}

If, hypothetically, $S_i\in\{0,1\}$ indicated inclusion with known $q_i>0$ for all $i=1,\ldots,N$, then
\begin{equation}
    \hat{A}_{\mathrm{HT}}(t) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{S_i\,\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}_{i,t}=y_i)}{q_i}
\end{equation}
is unbiased for $A_N(t)$ over the sampling design, with $\hat{y}_{i,t}$ fixed at $t$.

\begin{proof}
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{\mathrm{HT}}(t)] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \mathbb{I}(\hat{y}_{i,t}=y_i)\,\mathbb{E}[S_i]/q_i = A_N(t)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Monitoring objective and signal amplification}

The Horvitz--Thompson construction recovers $A_N(t)$ by reweighting included instances inversely with $q_i$, thereby mapping the monitoring measure back toward the uniform benchmark measure. That reweighting down-weights frequent error types on the disagreement-enriched panel when their inclusion probabilities are low: it is appropriate for unbiased population estimation.

For drift detection, the goal is different: maximise sensitivity to temporal changes in $\theta_t$ where the classifier is unstable. Large importance weights $1/q_i$ would suppress the contribution of boundary-heavy errors to the aggregate score, ``flattening'' the signal toward the global mean and reducing contrast for anomaly monitoring. Reporting the raw biased empirical accuracy $\hat{A}_n(t)$ (and confusion structure on $\mathcal{D}_n$) therefore acts as a high-gain monitoring signal on the fixed panel; the HT framework above still formalises how a positively designed subset would link to $A_N(t)$ if unbiased recovery were required.

The study uses unweighted $\hat{A}_n(t)$ and related confusion-based metrics on $\mathcal{D}_n$ (Section~\ref{sec:classification-metrics}) as leading indicators of drift, not as HT estimates of $A_N(t)$. If unbiased estimation of $A_N(t)$ alongside drift monitoring were required, one would add a separate random component (e.g.\ SRS from $\mathcal{D}_N$) with positive $q_i$ on all items of interest.
